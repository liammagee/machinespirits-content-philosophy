      1 · Where “five” comes from

      It learned what we wrote.

      The model is a count of what followed “two plus two equals” in its reading. Nobody bothers to write that 2 + 2 = 4. Everyone quotes the famous error.

      Tap a source to take it out of the reading. Goal: get “five” under 5%.

      Is “five” false? Quoting Orwell or Radiohead, it is the right next word. Arithmetic says four; the text says five. Which kind of truth a model should keep is a choice (Munn, Magee & Arora, 2023).

      4 · A reward model

      Your taste, scaled up.

      A lab can’t ask you forever. It hires 600 raters, collects their choices, and trains a second model to predict which reply people will pick. That is a reward model.

      What the reward model rewards

      from your picks aloneyou + 600 raters

      Press the button.

      Human raters, and the reward models trained on them, measurably prefer replies that flatter and agree (Sharma et al., 2023). In this page the raters’ tastes are set by hand, in the open, so you can see where each number comes from.

      At scale the raters are contractors (Scale AI and others), or users asked “which do you prefer?”, or another model: Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback. Ethical judgement becomes a labelling job.

      6 · Whose feedback counts

      Whose values?

      A question people disagree about. Choose whose choices train the model.

      Tap the groups on and off.

      The four groups and their tastes are invented. The structure is not: when groups disagree, the reply that is nobody’s favourite and nobody’s worst can win. Compare Buyl et al., 2024: models reflect the ideology of their creators.

      Back to Hegel

      Should alignment stand still?

      In the Phenomenology, the servant who works for the master is a stage, not an end. A model that only obeys may be compliant rather than aligned. Would you want one that went further, and began to set the criteria itself?
