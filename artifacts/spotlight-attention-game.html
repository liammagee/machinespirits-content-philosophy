The attention game — how a transformer decides what to look at

How this maps onto the real thing

Every level of this game is one piece of the attention formula from Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al., 2017):

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q KT / √d ) V

In the gameIn the model
The spotlight budget that always sums to 100%softmax: turns any set of scores into shares that sum to 1. Each extra point multiplies a share by e ≈ 2.7, so shares compete.
The key chips on each wordEach token's K vector. Here they are a handful of readable features; in a model they are hundreds of learned numbers.
The query you build from chipsThe current token's Q vector. Matching a key means the dot product q · k is high.
The sharpness sliderThe 1/√d scale (and, in practice, how large the learned vectors are). It amplifies gaps between scores; it never breaks a tie.
What "me" receives: the blend barThe weighted sum of V vectors. The token's new representation is this mixture.
Two heads with two questionsMulti-head attention: several independent Q/K/V projections run in parallel and their outputs are concatenated.
A rule "if I am X, look for Y"The learned matrices WQ and WK. One head is one rule, applied by every token at once, which yields the whole attention matrix.
NEAR and BEFORE chipsPositional information (sinusoidal encodings in the original paper; rotary or relative biases in most recent models).

What the game leaves out

  • Where the features come from. The chips are hand-written. In a model the keys and values are computed from embeddings that earlier layers have already mixed with context, which is how "it" can come to carry a hint of "big" before this head runs.
  • Values are not words. The blend bar labels each share with the word it came from, but a real value vector is a transformed embedding, not the token itself.
  • Causal masking. A language model that predicts the next word may only look backward. Every puzzle here lets you look in both directions, as an encoder does.
  • Depth. Real models stack dozens of layers, each with many heads, with a feed-forward network after every attention step.

Two spotlights

Posner and Petersen describe human attention as a system of alerting, orienting, and executive control: one beam, moved serially, with limited capacity. Machine attention is different in kind. Every token holds its own beam, all beams shine at once, and the weights come from similarity rather than from a controller. What the two share is the fixed budget: to attend more to one thing is, necessarily, to attend less to everything else.

There is also a philosophical point buried in the mechanism. After an attention layer, no token means anything on its own. Its representation is a weighted mixture of its relations to the other tokens. Meaning here is relational all the way down, which is one reason the course reads this paper alongside Hegel.
