How We Think, Chapter I

John Dewey · 1910 · Dispatch 2026-08-09

Dewey distinguishes reflective thought from the immediate acceptance of a suggestion. A difficulty interrupts the easy passage from one idea to a conclusion. Inquiry begins when judgment remains open while the thinker looks for facts that can test the suggested explanation.

What we are reading for

Read the discussion around pages 12–13 with three questions in view:

  1. Why is hesitation not simply the absence of thought?
  2. What turns uncertainty into directed inquiry rather than paralysis?
  3. How can a tutor ask for distinguishing evidence without making the learner perform an opaque diagnostic test?

Dewey is describing habits of reflective thought, not specifying an AI tutor architecture. The connection is therefore a design question: what interaction would let a learner participate in testing the tutor’s inference?

Dispatch

AI tutoring systems are encouraged to adapt quickly. That pressure can turn a single phrase into a learner label and a plausible interpretation into a fixed diagnosis. The system then personalises confidently around evidence it never actually obtained.

Suspended judgment suggests a different sequence. Name the uncertainty in ordinary language, keep only the alternatives that would change instruction, and ask a question whose answers lead to different next moves. Once evidence arrives, the tutor should commit provisionally and remain able to revise.

The point is not maximal caution. A tutor that endlessly lists possibilities also abandons the learner. The pedagogical skill is timed inquiry: delay closure when evidence is insufficient, obtain discriminating evidence, then act.
