How do you build a sense of your field and its gaps? This course aims to make sense of a term – academic literature – at once mundane and mystical. It starts with fundamentals: how to build, annotate, analyze, map and translate a literature database into a literature review. It progresses to think about how AI and other technology can help foster more critical, analytic and reflexive modes of interpretation and understandings of the field.

Anatomy of a Journal Article

  • Think back to Weber: the “Journal Article” is the vehicle of choice for transmitting science (German: Wissenschaft, or what we would call knowledge)
  • We read dozens, hundreds, thousands…
  • Our life is conditioned by the results of journal articles
    • For example, “AI” is today largely dominated by the effects of a single paper, “Attention is All You Need” (GS cites: > 275K)
    • Anthropic filed for IPO today: >$1.5tn
  • But what is a journal article? How do you analyze one? How do you write one?

Quick history

  • The “journal” is a recent(-ish) invention
  • Convenient communication of science (European enlightenment: 17-19th centuries; “disinterested” scholarship, “peer” review)
  • Carey, last week: peer review as “parlor game”
    • (Burke, Kenneth. The Philosophy of Literary Form: Studies in Symbolic Action. Louisiana State UP, 1941.)
  • But also literally: French 17th-18th century salons, “age of conversation”, rise of the “public sphere”
    • (Habermas, J. (1991). The structural transformation of the public sphere: An inquiry into a category of bourgeois society (T. Burger & F. Lawrence, Trans.). MIT Press. (Original work published 1962))
  • Universities as today’s “polite society”? What are the norms and standards of debate?

An “ontology” (or supply chain) of publishing

  • A collection of articles
  • Authors of the article
  • An editor (who reviews and assembles articles)
    • Peer reviewers (other scholars who the editor invites to review articles)
  • A publisher (who recruits the editor, typesets, sells and distributes the journal to readers)
  • A readership (who subscribes to the journal - very often university libraries)

What is the purpose of an article?

  • An article makes an argument
  • As such, it is a form of persuasive writing
    • It aims to change the mind of a reader
    • But to do so, it must use evidence (and other things)
  • All the parts of an article should contribute to this aim: persuasion
  • This is true of the literature review too…

Typical Structure of an Article (and Most Dissertations)

  • Introduction
  • Literature Review
  • Methodology
  • Findings
  • Results
  • Conclusion
  • But why? Why these parts and not others?

What is a literature review? The phenomenological view…

  • Your Introduction introduces your research question.
  • I’m sold - it intrigues me (the reader). But I know you’re not the first person to argue something like this…
  • In fact I saw another article like this just last week…
  • But you’re the expert. So tell me who has studied this before. Organize what you’re telling me:
  • It could be a history of your field…
  • Or a collection of related themes / topics…
  • Or a geographical survey…
  • Or it could be a path from the abstract (“According to Piaget, childhood development…”) that proceeds to the concrete (“Studies in Illinois over the past decade show a deficit in pre-K”)…
  • Great, now I know what others have said. But tell me: what are you going to add to that conversation?
  • Persuade me; tell me this has never been said before. Think of the parlor situation: is this something really new? or just gossip I’ve heard before?

Shark Tank?

  • If it helps: the literature review analyzes the “market” of ideas, and identifies a gap in that market

Does a Literature Review need its own Methodology?

  • We have a Research Question / Topic
  • What’s next? Just blast out a search on Google Scholar / University Website? Build a list of references, annotate, summarize, organize? Find the gap?
    • But can come across as arbitrary
  • Or: think of the Literature Review as a mini-research project within the project ( / article / dissertation)
  • Or an argument (“this is the gap…”) within a greater argument (“… and this is how I filled it”)
  • So it becomes beneficial to think of a methodology - a recipe or set of steps for how you compile the review

Learn from Marx?

  • Order of analysis is different from order of presentation
    • In analysis we go from concrete (stuff we see) to abstract (concepts of thought)
    • In presentation, we go from abstract (concepts) to concrete (the world of experience) - explaining the world of experience

What’s the “research question” of the Literature Review?

  • What’s the gap in the literature? (should eventually coincide with the research question)
    • What are you arguing? That this gap exists… (perhaps other things too)
  • Develop a Methodology:
    • Choose relevant search terms (think about AI’s ability to translate the terms you think of to the ones you don’t)
      • Pick a couple of relevant articles and read the references
    • Choose an organizing principle:
      • Organize by theme? What are the themes?
      • Organize by time? How have debates on topic X evolved?
  • Construct an Introduction and Conclusion

Metaphors of Knowledge: Navigating fields and finding gaps

  • What is the field? Talk your reader through it…
  • What is the gap? Tell your reader how you get there…
    • work backwards - find the gap, then organize the steps…
  • Use the language of argument / metadiscourse:
    • If we are on a “path” through the “field”, join the dots:
      • Move up / down: general - specific
        • “More generally…”, “At an abstract level”, “Theoretically…”, “Globally / Nationally…”
        • “More specifically…”, “At a concrete level”, “Practically”, “Locally…”
      • Move side to side: similar - different
        • “Similarly…”, “Along the same lines…”,
        • “However…”, “Conversely…”, “On the other hand…”, “In contrast…”
    • “Control” your navigation:
      • “This review will cover…”, “The literature describes several facets of…”
      • Add some drama: “Meanwhile…”, “Despite…”, “X has disputed Y’s findings…”
      • “This review has covered…”

Work it like a Puzzle

  • Look at the whole picture - does it make sense?
  • Fill it in piece by piece
  • Iterate - whole to part, then part to whole, then whole to part
  • More vocabulary: the Hermeneutic Circle
    • Understand the Whole (in order to Interpret the Part)
    • Interpret the Part (in order to Understand the Whole)

Common Pitfalls

  • The Annotated Bibliography and the Literature Review - what’s the difference?
  • You cannot convey all of the literature (a population); you get to sample selectively
  • Don’t persuade the reader you’ve read everything (you can’t); persuade the reader of the gap
  • Tone: Navigating the “Two Cultures” (see Snow 1959)
  • Lack of Signposting / metadiscourse / discourse markers / rhetorical statements
  • Lack of persuasive purpose (the “meandering review”)
  • “Laundry list” of gaps -> Narrow down on the one / two that you are likely to follow

More on tone…

  • Tone: Navigating The Two Cultures (see Snow 1959)
  • Scientist / Quantitative / Positivist
    • Lots of 3rd person / passive voice (“It was observed that…”) - But note, this is changing
  • Humanist / Qualitative / Post-positivist or Interpretivist or Constructivist
    • More 1st person / active voice (“We noticed that”)
  • In the field of education, both “cultures” co-exist (sometimes even peacefully)
  • How do you decide on your tone?

Does a Literature Review Need a Methodology?

Checklist:

  1. What are the key terms of the (draft) research question? List them…
  2. What are the norms of your field? And what is your field?
  3. What is a reasonable method or recipe for your literature review?
  4. What are related terms (can AI find reasonable synonyms)?
  5. What do you get when you search Google Scholar / library databases for these terms?
  6. Do you have existing articles / authors to draw from?
  7. What citation system are you using? Can you code / organize literature in the way you’d like?
  8. What is the best organization of the literature? Thematic, field (for multi-/interdisciplinary work), chronological, spatial, general / special?
  9. Are there classics you are expected to cite? References with very large number of citations (e.g. Braun & Clarke 2006; Vaswani et al. 2017; Bender et al. 2021)?

Growing your database

Different techniques for expanding your database:

  • Search (Google Scholar / library databases)
  • With existing articles:
    • Go back: Scan references of this article
    • Go forward: Search for citations of this article
    • Go sideways: What else was published in the same journal / issue
  • Check common “authorities”: Wikipedia, syllabi, AI
    • e.g. “Critical AI” - people will be citing Bender et al. (2021) stochastic parrots paper
  • Investigate: Who are the classics that shaped a field? Who are the contemporaries that are re-shaping the field?
  • Water cooler conversation: what names keep appearing in lecture notes, conference papers, other student essays, discussions?
  • Tools:
    • Connected Papers
    • Semantic Scholar
    • ResearchRabbit
    • Testing AI academic search engines
  • Other tips & tricks?

Population to Sample to Sub-sample

  • Your database is a sample of a population
  • Your review will (usually) be a sample of that sample
  • What determines what to include? what to exclude? How do you determine relevance?
  • No hard answers but consider:
  • Classics in the field
  • Important recent contributions (last 2 years)
  • Exemplars of perspectives (Chomsky vs Pinker) - not the middle ground, not the commentaries
  • What fits your narrative - but be cautious about cherry picking. Lean into - rather than avoid - the inconvenient literature!
  • Be explicit about what is excluded and why

Do some sketches: pilot the review

  • Experiment with different organizing principles: Time, followed by theme, followed by space
  • Just be approximate

“Criticism of AI dates back to the inception of the field itself. For example, Dreyfus (1965) discussed the brittleness of (etc)… By the early 1980s, scepticism voiced by philosophers such as Searle (1980) in his famous”Chinese room” experiment, contributed to the emergence of an AI winter. More recently, as generative AI has largely revived the field, criticism has been directed toward both issues of social justice and bias (e.g. Bender et al. 2021) and potential existential risk (e.g. lesswrong blog). In some cases, critical AI scholars have also argued the existential case is a convenient disguise that obscures more immediate negative social effects (Gebru and Torres 2024). Moreover scholars from outside the US - particularly in the Global South – have begun to argue AI constitutes a form of neocolonialism, impacting digital sovereignty (Keerthiraj & Misra, 2025)”

  • Can work with or without AI - useful just to test how this or that understanding helps make your case (about gaps)
  • Don’t commit to this structure just yet!

Then develop a plan…

  1. Tentative gap - what do you think it is?
  2. Scaffold your review - how many sections? do you need subsections? Add preliminary citations
  3. Develop a sense of key transitions - what’s the drama in the review? “also argued”, “moreover” etc
  4. Be light, noncommittal - prepared to cut/paste
  5. As your literature grows, you might need to qualify the statement of the gap. Maybe there are studies of the general topic X - but you can add adjectives to the gap (research question) to narrow it down… Go back to Step 1, rinse and repeat…

Gap Finder: refine your question until it meets a gap

  • Gap Finder

What you are doing: testing a research question against the published literature, one change at a time, to find where a review could add something.

  • Goal: a question that fewer than 25 articles answer, with plenty of research nearby
  • Steps
    1. Start from How is generative AI used in literature reviews? Thousands of articles answer it: crowded
    2. Refine: add a concept on the right (who, which practice, which issue, where) to narrow the question; remove one to broaden it
    3. Check: click a ball to list a sample of its zone’s articles. Are they really about your question?
    4. Your turn: when you reach a gap, read the draft gap statement, then choose Write your own and try your own question
  • Open Gap Finder
  • It searches titles and abstracts of journal articles since 2022 in OpenAlex. The matching is rough (“supervision” also finds banking supervision): treat the verdict as a prompt to read, not a finding

Reading the field

  • Inner sphere: the articles that answer your question, combining all your concepts. Its outline colour is the verdict: red for crowded, green for a gap, yellow for too thin
  • Outer shell: the articles nearby, which combine all but one of your concepts
  • A gap is a nearly empty centre inside a full shell: plenty of research on the parts, little on the combination. If the shell is nearly empty too, the question may be too narrow, or a concept goes by another name in this field
  • Every ball counts the same: all the balls are the same size and each stands for the same number of articles (the key in the corner says how many), so how full a zone is shows how much research there is. Balls are placed at random: clusters mean nothing

How was Gap Finder made?

As with the class constellation, it was built with Claude over several conversations. This prompt is a starting point for a similar game:

Build a single-file interactive HTML game for a graduate seminar on literature reviews that shows how a research question and its literature shape each other. Search OpenAlex through a small server route that holds the API key. The student builds a question by adding or removing concepts (topic, who, which practice, which issue, where), or types their own question and the game picks out its concepts. For each version of the question, count the journal articles whose title or abstract combines every concept, and those that combine all but one. Show the field in 3D with three.js as two zones of identical glass balls placed at random: an inner sphere for the articles that combine every concept, outlined in the colour of the verdict, and an outer shell for those that combine all but one. Each ball stands for the same number of articles, so how full a zone is shows how much research there is; clicking a ball lists a sample of that zone’s articles. The goal is to refine the question until fewer than 25 articles answer it directly while many sit nearby. Record each change as a version on a visible path, and when the goal is met, list the papers to read and draft a signposted gap statement.

Also consider…

  • Different perspectives - outside the ordinary frame of discipline, time
    • e.g. “heteroscopia” (“many visions”) - concept by Jaireth (2001) to describe Indian cinema
    • But also a useful term for thinking about machinic vision, generative AI
  • Less-known authors (especially not widely cited) - this can enrich a review, demonstrating greater awareness beyond the same few authors / studies…
    • e.g. History of AI - typically focussed on US developments. But Schmidhuber (2022) argues the importance of developments in Ukraine (formerly part of the USSR), Japan, Finland in the 1960s/70s. This shows depth, a willingness to step outside conventions
    • But don’t lean too heavily on exotic sources.
  • In short: surprise us (but within our Zone of Proximal Development)!

This Week’s Activity: Task 2 · Review design method and scope (10%)

  1. Write down your assumed audience / field. What do you know about them? Do they prefer lots of recent citations (science)? Or fewer, with more emphasis on the classics (philosophy, theory, some qual studies)?
  2. Create a short plan / recipe method. Include things like:
    • “Search for these terms / filters”
    • “Create a database of 10 articles”
    • “Scan the articles for relevance - discard those that are not relevant”
    • “With remaining articles: review references, search for more recent citations”
    • “Once I have 50 articles: code / annotate for relevance”
    • “Map out review structure; add citations, continue to search under subheadings”
    • “Supply list of refs to AI and ask if I’m missing anything crucial”
  3. Write down what you imagine or hope will be the gap in the literature (should be close to your research question). Importantly: what would evidence of a gap look like? (this is of course a hard question).

The purpose of the plan / recipe is that it should (ideally) be reproducible by others. Does it pass that test? Next week: share the recipe with others - could they “bake” a literature review to answer the question?
