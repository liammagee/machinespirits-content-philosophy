Course description

How do we conduct scholarly research in an era of generative AI? This graduate course combines the fundamentals of a rigorous literature review with a critical examination of AI-assisted scholarship. You will develop a research question, locate and organize relevant literature, read closely and across a field, and turn that work into a persuasive scholarly argument.

The practical work and the critical questions belong together. AI can produce a summary, propose a conceptual map, or suggest a gap. We will ask how those outputs were made, whose knowledge they privilege, what they miss, and what responsibility remains with the researcher. The aim is to develop better scholarly judgment while learning to use these tools.

Catalog listing. EPOL 590 is listed as Advanced Graduate Seminar (0–4 hours; may be repeated to a maximum of 12 hours if topics vary). This section, AIL, is restricted to graduate students at Urbana-Champaign. Its catalog description: “This course aims to make sense of a term – academic literature – at once mundane and mystical. It starts with fundamentals: how to build, annotate, analyze, map and translate a literature database into a literature review. It progresses to think about how AI and other technology can help foster more critical, analytic and reflexive modes of interpretation and understandings of the field.”

Course outline

The course develops a cumulative literature review through seven connected activities:

  1. Question — define a significant, feasible research topic and provisional question.
  2. Design — set the review’s purpose, scope, search approach, and audience.
  3. Map — identify relationships, disagreements, and gaps in the literature.
  4. Interpret and write — read difficult texts and compare scientific and humanities writing conventions.
  5. Structure — organize a synthesis and guide the reader with framing and signposting.
  6. Reflect — examine positionality, knowledge communities, and AI’s role in the work.
  7. Synthesize — revise a complete review and defend its contribution.

You may work on a dissertation, journal article, or another research project. An exploratory or “mock” review is also welcome. The task is to learn how to build and revise a defensible account of a field; the final document may serve another purpose, but it does not have to.

The review is provisional throughout. A map can change the question. A difficult text can change the categories. Writing can reveal that the apparent gap was the wrong one. Keep those changes visible.

Course objectives

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  1. Develop and revise a significant, feasible research question through engagement with literature.
  2. Design a transparent approach to finding, selecting, and organizing sources.
  3. Use close reading and mapping to analyze relationships, disagreements, omissions, and possible contributions.
  4. Write a coherent synthesis appropriate to a scholarly audience, with effective structure, citation, and signposting.
  5. Explain how positionality and knowledge communities shape research questions and interpretation.
  6. Evaluate AI-assisted research outputs, verify their sources, and account for how AI influenced the work.

Baseline reflection

Before or during the first seminar, write a short, ungraded account of your project:

  • What question do you want to investigate, and why does it matter?
  • What do you already know about the relevant literature?
  • What would change your current understanding?
  • How have you used AI in research, and what are you uncertain about?

This reflection establishes a starting point for comparison with your final work.

Weekly research and revision cycle

For each staged task:

  1. Read and annotate relevant course material and literature in your field.
  2. Try the week’s method on your own project.
  3. Check sources and claims, including any suggested by AI.
  4. Bring a question, draft, or map to the workshop.
  5. Revise the relevant part of your review.
  6. Submit the task with a brief account of what changed and how AI contributed, if used.

Self-reflection helps explain your decisions. It does not automatically determine your grade. The 479 self-grading scheme and separate peer-commentary components have been replaced by 590’s staged assignments and two graded peer reviews.

1 Research topic and question

This task is ungraded. It is your first chance to get feedback on your question before any marks are at stake. State a provisional question and explain its significance, feasibility, and relationship to an initial set of sources. Identify what you would need to read or discover to refine it.

2 Review design method and scope

Explain the review’s purpose, audience, boundaries, search approach, selection criteria, and proposed organization. Distinguish an exploratory course review from any claim to have completed a systematic review. Include enough detail for a reader to understand how the collection was assembled.

3 Conceptual map of the literature

Represent the main concepts, debates, approaches, or historical developments in your field. Link important relationships to sources and identify uncertainties or omissions. Accompany the map with a short explanation of how it affects your question and search.

4 Literature review writing sample

Develop a section that synthesizes literature rather than describing articles one at a time. Compare scientific and humanities modes of framing or interpretation; explain which choices serve your question and audience. Submit one 250–400-word section with a few sentences of commentary on your choices of audience, voice and evidence.

5 Structured draft and signposting

Submit an outline and a developed draft section showing the review’s organizing argument. Use headings, transitions, and metadiscourse to make relationships explicit. Explain one structural revision and how it improves the reader’s understanding.

6 AI-use statement and reflexive commentary

Explain where AI influenced searching, reading, mapping, or writing; what you checked; what you rejected; and what responsibility you retained. Connect your position as a researcher to a specific choice in the review. Include selected examples where useful, without requiring publication of private conversations or irrelevant personal information.

7 Final literature review or course outline

Choose one of two forms. Both build on the staged work.

  • Option A: a literature review. Submit a coherent review that develops a defensible contribution from the staged work. It should establish a question and scope, synthesize relevant literature, explain relationships and disagreements, and identify a plausible gap or a different way to frame the problem.
  • Option B: a course outline for teaching how to write a literature review. Design a short course or module that teaches a specific audience to write a literature review in your field. Set out each session’s aim, readings, activities and how learning is checked. Justify the design with the course readings and wider literature, including where AI belongs in the process and where it does not. Like a review, the outline needs a clear scope, sources that support its choices, and an argument for why the sequence works.

Either option is 1,500–2,000 words. Include an AI-use statement and a short revision memo explaining how the review changed in response to evidence and feedback. Use one consistent author–date citation style (APA 7th edition unless your field uses another). Submit a Google Doc link or a file. The revision memo is 250–350 words. The rubric is on Canvas.

The final seminar is a workshop for this submission. Aim to arrive with a complete working draft. The confirmed 15 December deadline allows revision after that workshop.

Assessment criteria

The common criteria are:

  • significance and feasibility of the question;
  • relevance and traceability of sources;
  • quality of interpretation and synthesis;
  • clarity of organization, framing, and scholarly voice;
  • reflexivity and responsible, transparent AI use;
  • responsiveness to evidence and feedback.

The staged tasks emphasize development and reasoned decisions. The final review emphasizes how those decisions work together in the completed argument. Each task is marked on Canvas with a rubric built on these six criteria.

Peer commentary

Peer workshops run in Sessions 4 and 6, with instructor feedback on the map and structured draft. After you submit Tasks 4 and 6, Canvas assigns you classmates’ submissions to review. Each peer review is worth 5%. It is graded complete or incomplete: complete means specific, constructive comments on each assigned submission by the review deadline (Friday 20 November for Task 4, Friday 11 December for Task 6). The Task 6 reviews arrive in time to help with the final review.

Share only the material needed for the exercise. Use named recipients and institution-supported arrangements. Students may use an anonymized extract or an instructor-mediated alternative. Keep grades and private instructor comments in Canvas. Canvas assigns each student two classmates’ submissions once the task’s deadline passes. If you would rather not share your draft, tell me before the deadline and we will use an anonymized extract or route the exchange through me.

Use of AI in this course

AI is an object of study and a possible research tool. Use it in ways that make your own judgment more visible.

  • Explore interpretations, test arguments, compare organizational choices, and generate search leads.
  • Verify citations against the actual source. Read enough to establish what the source supports.
  • Describe consequential AI use in each submission; retain selected examples of useful outputs and errors.
  • Distinguish the author’s claim, your interpretation, and an AI-generated suggestion.
  • Do not present fabricated references, unverified quotations, or generated analysis you cannot explain as established scholarship.

A polished output is not sufficient evidence of learning. Be prepared to explain why a source belongs in the review, how a claim follows from it, and what you changed. This section is the course AI policy. The University’s academic-integrity rules also apply; see Academic integrity under Policies.
