Lecture 3 — Workshop guide: Two articles, two scholarly traditions

EPOL 590: AI and Academic Literature | Monday, November 2, 2026 | 5:00–7:50 p.m. Central

Instructor working copy. This is a separate workshop companion to lecture-3.md, which holds the slide deck.

Purpose

Students compare how two academic articles construct arguments, establish evidence, and position themselves in a scholarly field. They first interpret a text independently, then test AI-assisted readings, then teach the other cohort what they found. The aim is not simply to produce summaries but to notice what different scholarly traditions count as convincing and whether AI preserves those distinctions.

Seed articles

The two articles illustrate C. P. Snow’s “two cultures”. Both address the same concern, aligning AI with human values, from opposite sides: one reads a field’s literature to ask what “values” means, the other runs experiments on what preference-based training does.

Humanities / critical studies: Smart, A., Ahmed, S., Kay, J., Tobin, J., Shrishak, K., & Birhane, A. (2026), “Toward a Theory of Value in AI Alignment,” arXiv:2608.10327 (preprint). https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10327 The method is qualitative: the authors annotated 94 value-alignment papers to recover the theory of value they assume.

Computer science (alignment): Sharma, M., Tong, M., Korbak, T., Duvenaud, D., Askell, A., Bowman, S. R., et al. (2024), “Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models,” International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548 — read the main paper (about 9 pages); the appendices are optional.

Licensing: Both articles are open access under CC BY 4.0, so students may download them, share them, and upload the full PDFs to AI tools. They replace the Lecture 2 seed articles (Keerthiraj & Misra; Liu et al.), which are subscription content whose library licences do not clearly permit uploading to AI services. No paid AI account required.

Shared record: Google Docs

Google Docs is the authoritative record for all three workshops — notes, reading tables, handover briefs, discoveries, maps, and reflections. No Canvas, no Google Sheets, no other documentation platform. Avoid entering the same data twice.

Prepare one Doc (or tab) per group: Doc A — Humanities group (reads Smart et al. first) and Doc B — Computer Science group (reads Sharma et al. first). In the class Doc these are the DocA and DocB tabs under Week 3 Collaboration. Each contains:

  1. Both article links (the group’s own article, and the one it searches from in Hour 2), group roster/recorder, chosen reading strategies.
  2. Hour 1 reading table: reader/strategy; claim; evidence with passage; uncertainty; AI comparison; what changed.
  3. Handover brief (copy/paste into the other group’s Doc): central claim; two unresolved questions; one promising reference; source passage(s).
  4. Hour 2 discovery log: publication/DOI; backward, forward, or sideways discovery; conventional vs AI suggestion; verified?; significance to the original article. Each group logs its discoveries about the other group’s article in its own Doc.
  5. Hour 3 mapping: links to initial and revised maps (or embedded images); map type; labeled relationships; peer critique; possible gaps and how to verify them; revision rationale.
  6. Final reflection: Did AI deepen reading and scholarship or just accelerate production?

Each group nominates a recorder, and optionally a separate workbench operator. Project the shared Docs during the first two workshops and the maps during the third. The Machine Spirits literature workbench is optional, for PDFs, visualizations, and personal experimentation; it is not the record.

5:00–5:15 — AI research workbench setup (15 minutes)

Goal: Everyone can work with at least one of the two assigned papers in an AI environment and verify a passage-based answer. No paid account, terminal installation, or unrestricted file access required.

Illinois-supported starting points: Consult the Illinois AI Apps directory and Illinois Library guidance before class. Recommend Google NotebookLM for source-grounded comparison of documents and Gemini through eligible Illinois Google accounts; Microsoft Copilot Chat and Illinois Chat are alternatives. Availability and entitlements vary by account; students should verify sign-in and access. ChatGPT Edu/Codex access should not be assumed for every student. Students with existing ChatGPT or Claude subscriptions may use them for permitted, non-sensitive material; personal subscriptions are not a substitute for institutional data protection. Claude Code and Codex are optional advanced file-oriented routes, not required installations.

Important distinction: A desktop application can still call a cloud model. A browser app can work with multiple PDFs when its upload or source-collection feature permits. A local-folder-capable agent requires deliberate permissions. Do not imply NotebookLM automatically reads arbitrary desktop folders.

Before class: Share both article links (or the PDFs themselves). Because both are CC BY, full-PDF upload to NotebookLM or another tool is permitted; for any other paper a student brings, check the licence first and fall back to a short excerpt. Encourage students to create an EPOL590-literature folder, but don’t require any particular operating system or software installation.

  • 5:00–5:03 — Inventory: Quick poll: NotebookLM, Gemini, Illinois Chat, Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, other, none? Browser or desktop? Can students access the article text? Pair students for support.
  • 5:03–5:08 — Demonstration: Open NotebookLM using an eligible Illinois account, add a permitted source (article text or excerpt), and ask: “What is the central claim? Cite the supporting passage, and say if the source does not answer.” Open and verify the citation in the actual source. Explain that source citations still need checking.
  • 5:08–5:13 — Student setup: Students choose an available Illinois-supported route first, or an existing personal subscription for permitted material. Upload/add the relevant article or paste an excerpt. Advanced students may use a file-aware tool with access limited to the course-paper folder; no one needs to install a CLI in class.
  • 5:13–5:15 — Smoke test: Everyone obtains one claim with a verifiable source passage, or switches to a peer/browser fallback. Record “ready / partner / fallback”; proceed on time.

Safety and accessibility: Do not upload student records, unpublished research, restricted publisher PDFs, or confidential information without appropriate authorization. Use approved institutional accounts where required. If access or uploads fail, students can read the paper and critically compare peer-generated interpretations instead. Offer equivalent participation without a paid AI service.

Hour 1 — Reading strategies workshop (flexible 5:15–6:20)

Attendance-flexible arrangement: Form two article groups, not six triads. Group A, the Humanities group, reads Smart et al.; Group B, the Computer Science group, reads Sharma et al. If attendance is large enough, each article group can split into two subgroups, but the exercise works with as few as two students per article. If attendance is extremely low, read one article together and use the second as a comparison demonstrated by the instructor.

Experimental variable: Students approach the same article using different reading strategies. They answer the same questions and record the same evidence. This is a structured comparison of reading practices, not a controlled experiment or a race to finish an entire paper.

5:15–5:20 — Predict and choose (5 min)

Briefly revisit Lecture 2’s question: What do we do when we open an article? Within each article group, students select or are assigned one of three approaches. With only two students, choose two strategies and discuss the third as an alternative.

  1. Sequential: Begin at the abstract and move through the paper in its published order. Track how the argument develops; do not pretend a long article can be fully read in twelve minutes.
  2. Strategic / overview-first: Read abstract, introduction, conclusion, then jump to sections needed to test the claimed contribution.
  3. Investigative / question-first: Start at methodology or results (either article: Sharma’s experiments, Smart’s annotation scheme), or at the references or a contested passage. Work outward to reconstruct the whole.

Each student predicts which strategy will yield the strongest interpretation, which will be fastest, and which will raise the most questions.

5:20–5:33 — Human reading (13 min; no AI)

Students independently read using their chosen route and note where they started and what they skipped. Everyone produces the same four-part reading record:

  • Claim: What is the paper trying to persuade its readers to believe?
  • Warrant: What evidence, reasoning, method, or examples support it? Give a passage or section reference.
  • Uncertainty: What remains unclear or unconvincing?
  • Reading path: What did your chosen route reveal or conceal?

Do not penalize incomplete readings; make their incompleteness part of the analysis.

5:33–5:45 — Compare strategies within each article group (12 min)

Each student reports their strategy, interpretation, and one cited passage. Compare where accounts converge or conflict. Ask: Which disagreement comes from the article, and which comes from where we entered it? Nominate one spokesperson per article.

5:45–6:00 — AI follows the same reading route (15 min)

Students use NotebookLM, Gemini, or another permitted AI tool, supplying the actual article or allowed excerpt. Ask AI to follow the same strategy they used. If the tool has only an excerpt, explicitly constrain claims to it.

Sequential prompt: “Read the supplied text in its presented order. At each section, record how the central argument develops, which evidence is added, and what remains unresolved. Cite passages. Do not fill in missing sections.”

Overview-first prompt: “Start with the abstract, introduction, and conclusion. State your provisional interpretation, then identify the specific additional sections that would most seriously test it. Read those sections and revise the interpretation, citing passages.”

Investigative prompt: “Begin with [named section/question]. Explain what that section establishes, then trace backward and forward to reconstruct the article’s larger argument. Distinguish what you verified from what you inferred.”

Common challenge: “Compare this reading with my human notes. Identify one insight, one unsupported or flattened interpretation, and one question worth pursuing. Quote or cite supporting passages; do not invent page numbers.”

Students record what changed when AI followed their path rather than simply requesting a generic summary. An AI-free alternative is a peer applying the same prompts.

6:00–6:15 — Exchange across articles (15 min)

Bring the two groups together. Each spokesperson gives a 3-minute account; reserve time for questions and comparison. Ask:

  • Did the humanities and computer science articles reward different reading routes?
  • Both articles are about aligning AI with human values. Sharma et al. treat human preferences as the signal; Smart et al. ask whether preferences can stand in for values. Where do they agree, and what does each culture’s method let it see that the other misses?
  • Which strategies exposed the authors’ standards of evidence and persuasion?
  • Did AI preserve disciplinary differences or impose a generic template?
  • How did reading the part alter understanding of the whole, and vice versa (the hermeneutic circle)?

Output: A two-column comparison of article/reading strategy, plus one shared caution about AI reading.

6:15–6:20 — Synthesis and transition (5 min)

Invite students to revise their predictions. Distinguish reading for information extraction from reading to develop an interpretation. Ask: “If AI can traverse a paper in many orders, what makes our practice of reading scholarly?”

Each article group then writes its handover brief in its Doc — central claim, two unresolved questions, one promising reference, and the supporting source passage(s) — ready to pass to the other group after the break.

Time policy: This first workshop runs to 6:20, intentionally crossing the original hour boundary. Break 6:20–6:30; the second workshop starts at 6:30. If discussion runs long, take the time from the break or the 7:15 mapping demonstration, not from the student comparison or the groups’ mapping time.

Hour 2 — Literature discovery workshop (6:30–7:15)

Swap the articles. Each group now searches outward from the article it did not read, starting from the other group’s handover brief. Do not identify gaps in this hour — gaps belong to mapping, where they can be tested.

  • 6:30–6:35 — Swap and receive the brief: Copy each handover brief into the receiving group’s Doc. Readers clarify the claim and questions in a sentence or two.
  • 6:35–6:45 — Conventional search: Trace citations backward (the article’s references), forward (later work citing it), and sideways (dissenting, heterodox, or adjacent positions). Log each find in the discovery log.
  • 6:45–7:00 — AI-assisted search: Repeat with a permitted AI tool. Verify every suggested reference exists and says what is claimed; mark AI suggestions separately from conventional finds. Select three useful works.
  • 7:00–7:10 — Report back: Each group reports its discoveries to the article’s original readers, who reconsider their Hour 1 interpretation in light of them.
  • 7:10–7:15 — Consolidate: Record verified discoveries only. No gap analysis yet.

Hour 3 — Mapping workshop (7:15–7:50)

Each group returns to its original article and maps the field emerging around it, using its own reading plus the other group’s discoveries.

  • 7:15–7:20 — Demonstration: Briefly show three views of the same literature: chronological, conceptual, and relational.
  • 7:20–7:32 — Map: Groups map their original article’s emerging field, labeling relationships between works.
  • 7:32–7:40 — Exchange and critique: Groups swap maps and question the relationships. Where do possible gaps appear?
  • 7:40–7:46 — Revise: Revise the map and record why.
  • 7:46–7:50 — Final reflection: Is an absence a gap in the field, or a gap in the map — an artifact of search or representation? Return to vibe scholarship: did AI deepen reading and scholarship, or just accelerate production?

What to collect

Everything goes in the group’s Google Doc tab.

  • Hour 1, each student: article, chosen reading path, prediction, claim, warrant with source passage, uncertainty, and what AI changed (reading table).
  • Hour 1, each article group: one point of agreement and one divergence across strategies, plus the handover brief.
  • Hour 1, whole class: one difference between disciplinary reading practices and one question to investigate through literature search.
  • Hour 2, each group: the discovery log in its own Doc, with three verified works selected.
  • Hour 3, each group: initial and revised maps, peer critique, possible gaps and how to verify them, revision rationale, and the final reflection.

Facilitator contingencies

  • Small turnout: Two article groups; no requirement for three strategies per group. Students can compare routes verbally or in pairs.
  • Very small turnout: One group jointly compares the two articles, with students choosing contrasting reading routes.
  • Incomplete or inaccessible papers: Use permitted excerpts and mark interpretations provisional.
  • AI access problems: Compare human readings; the pedagogical objective survives without an AI tool.
  • Timing: Do not rush the cross-article exchange merely to preserve a 6:00 boundary.
