## Overall assessment

The course has a coherent intellectual and practical progression: question, review design, mapping, interpretation and writing, structure, reflexivity, and synthesis. Its strongest organizing principle is that scholarship develops through revision: reading changes the question, writing changes the map, and reflection changes what the researcher regards as evidence. AI belongs inside that cycle as something to examine and evaluate.

The imported lectures retain traces of how the 2025 course evolved in practice. Several introductory schedules describe different plans, and later sessions repeat material without explaining how the second encounter advances the work. The 2026 syllabus provides a clearer sequence. Retain that sequence and give each seminar one visible contribution to the cumulative review.

It then asks students to draft a topic, search around it, and ask which parts are already answered and which remain.

The current sequence is:

6. An experimental workshop approach, peer work, and the historical role of technology in knowledge.

9. The reciprocal relationship between a question and a literature review.
10. Practicality, novelty, and consequences.
11. Breakout discussion: choose and document a candidate topic.
12. Readings: Weber's *Science as a Vocation* and a University of Minnesota research-question guide.

## Monday teaching brief

### The central message

A good research question is a provisional commitment. It becomes more precise as we discover what others have studied, how they have framed the problem, and what their evidence can support. AI can help us generate alternatives and challenge our assumptions; scholarly judgment consists in deciding which alternatives deserve investigation and checking why.

### Outcomes

By the end of the seminar, students should be able to:

- distinguish a broad topic, an empirical research question, and a review question;
- explain why a question matters and what makes its scope workable;
- revise a question after considering a source or a peer's objection;
- identify an AI suggestion that needs verification;
- retain a short record of what changed and why.

### Opening words

“Over the next seven meetings, you will develop one literature review through several versions. You can bring a dissertation topic, another project, or a question you are still exploring. You do not need a perfect question tonight. You do need a question that can become better through reading.

“We will use AI to test possibilities and examine how scholarly work is changing. A fluent answer is a starting point for scrutiny. We will keep asking: where does this claim come from, what does it leave out, and what would make us revise it?”

### Mini-lecture and worked example

**A topic names an area:** AI feedback in first-year college writing.

**An empirical research question asks about the world:** How do first-year students decide whether to accept or reject AI-generated feedback on their writing?

**A review question asks what existing scholarship lets us establish:** How does the literature on AI-supported writing feedback define and investigate students' agency in revision?

These are illustrative questions, not claims that a gap has already been established.

Ask the class:

- What does “agency” mean here: accepting advice, making informed choices, retaining authorship, or something else?
- Which populations and educational settings belong in the review?
- Is better immediate writing the same outcome as learning to write?
- What kinds of evidence would answer the empirical question?
- What could a review establish without collecting new student data?
- Which adjacent literatures might use different terms?

Possible search leads include “automated writing evaluation,” “feedback literacy,” and “revision decisions.” Treat these as leads to test.

A more focused provisional review question might be:

> How do studies of AI-supported feedback in first-year college writing define student agency, and what evidence do they use to examine students' revision decisions?

Explain what improved: a clearer setting, a more specific phenomenon, and a distinction between definitions and evidence. Explain what remains open: whether the available literature supports this scope. Students may need to broaden, narrow, or reframe after searching.

### Five questions for evaluating a draft

1. **Meaning:** Can a reader tell what the central terms mean?
2. **Significance:** Who would care about the answer, and why?
3. **Scope:** Is there a workable population, context, period, or conceptual boundary?
4. **Evidence:** What sources or research could support an answer?
5. **Revisability:** What might make you change the question?

Novelty belongs in that conversation, but “nobody has studied this exact new product” is an insufficient reason by itself. A contribution may clarify a disagreement, expose an assumption, connect separated literatures, revisit a finding, or develop a different interpretation.

### A bounded AI exercise

Use the same worked example rather than opening an unrelated tool demonstration. The prompt is portable across available tools:

> I am developing a literature review question about AI-supported feedback in first-year college writing. My provisional question is: How do studies define student agency, and what evidence do they use to examine students' revision decisions?
>
> Ask three questions that would help me clarify the scope. Then suggest two alternative review questions and explain the trade-off in each. Suggest search terms, including terms from adjacent literatures. Do not invent references or claim a gap exists. Mark assumptions that require checking. Do not write the review.

Students identify one suggestion worth trying, one assumption requiring evidence, and one change they would decline. Check a relevant claim against an article students already have or one selected by the instructor. Record whether only the abstract or the full text was consulted.

**Fallback without a live tool:** give students these instructor-written suggestions, explicitly labeled as examples rather than actual model output:

- “Narrow the question to first-year writing.” Discuss what this improves and what literature it excludes.
- “Measure success by improvement in the submitted essay.” Ask whether that matches a question about agency or learning.
- “No research has examined student agency in AI feedback.” Identify the evidence needed before making this claim.

The same exercise works through peer critique. A tool account is unnecessary for participation in this version of the lesson.

### Project workshop instructions

Students work in groups of three, with roughly seven minutes per project.

Each presenter supplies a question and a two-sentence rationale. Peers ask about one ambiguous term, one scope decision, and one evidence need. They suggest a revision and explain its cost as well as its benefit. The presenter retains authority over the revised question.

Use a document students already know how to edit. Each student records:

| Item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Topic | |
| Initial research or review question | |
| Why it matters | |
| What the review could establish | |
| Source or search lead | |
| Peer or AI suggestion | |
| Revised question | |
| Reason for the change | |
| Next source to locate or check | |

No personal or confidential research data are needed. Students can use an illustrative topic.

### Link to the first assignment

The current unpublished Task 1 draft proposes **400–600 words plus 3–5 initial scholarly sources**, due **Friday 23 October at 11:59 PM Central**, worth **10%**. These specifications remain instructor choices.

The seminar output provides its structure: provisional question, significance, feasible scope, initial sources and their relevance, and what needs further investigation. A brief AI-use note records any contribution; “No AI used” is an acceptable description for this proposed exercise.

Keep the promise modest: students start the task in class and complete the reading, source checking, and revision afterward. The seminar cannot encompass most of the entire course's assessed work.

### Closing words and exit ticket

“Your question is allowed to change. The important thing is to be able to explain the change: a source contradicted an assumption, a peer exposed ambiguity, or the available literature made a different boundary more useful.”

Before leaving, students write:

1. My question at the start was …
2. It is now …
3. The most important reason for the change is …
4. My next reading or verification step is …
