## Session 1 · Research questions and academic literature

- **Required, from lectures.** Weber, M. (1946). Science as a vocation. In H. H. Gerth & C. W. Mills (Eds. & Trans.), *From Max Weber: Essays in sociology* (pp. 129–156). Oxford University Press.
  The lecture currently cites "*Science and the Quest for Reality* (Palgrave), pp. 382–394", which is a later anthology reprint. Cite whichever edition you post. Gerth and Mills is the standard source.
- **Required, from lectures.** University of Minnesota Libraries. (n.d.). *Characteristics of a good research question*. Literature Searching research guide. https://libguides.umn.edu/c.php?g=1337354&p=9854773

## Session 2 · Article anatomy, review design and mapping a field

- **Required, suggested.** Grant, M. J., & Booth, A. (2009). A typology of reviews: An analysis of 14 review types and associated methodologies. *Health Information & Libraries Journal, 26*(2), 91–108. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1471-1842.2009.00848.x
  This supports Task 2's distinction between an exploratory course review and a systematic review.
- **Required, from lectures.** Purdue Online Writing Lab. (n.d.). *Writing a literature review*. Purdue University. https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/conducting_research/writing_a_literature_review.html
- **In-class examples, from lectures.** These are the two articles the class takes apart in the session:
  - Keerthiraj, & Misra, A. (2025). Who owns the future? AI, digital sovereignty, and the politics of knowledge. *AI & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-025-02641-y
    *Verify:* the first author appears with one name only; check the published byline, volume and pages.
  - Liu, J., Zhang, Y., Li, W., Wang, Q., Niu, P., & Zhang, X. (2025). Adaptive vs. planned metacognitive scaffolding for computational thinking: Evidence from generative AI-supported programming in elementary education. *Computers & Education*, Article 105473.
    *Verify:* volume number. The lecture links a ScienceDirect URL with a personal access token in it; replace it with the DOI or a library link. Lectures 5 and 6 call this "Zhang et al. (2025)"; it should be "Liu et al. (2025)".

## Session 3 · AI-assisted close and distant reading

- **Required, from lectures.** Moretti, F. (2013). *Distant reading*. Verso.
  A suggested excerpt: "Conjectures on world literature" and "Graphs, maps, trees". Check the chapter titles and pages in your copy.
- **Optional, from lectures.** Snow, C. P. (1959). *The two cultures and the scientific revolution*. Cambridge University Press.
  Lecture 2 lists this as "next week's reading" through a sciencepolicy.colorado.edu PDF. It fits Session 4's scientific versus humanities writing just as well. Move it there if Session 3 is full.

## Session 4 · Difficult texts, audiences and scholarly voice

- **Required, from lectures.** Spivak, G. C. (1988). Can the subaltern speak? In C. Nelson & L. Grossberg (Eds.), *Marxism and the interpretation of culture* (pp. 271–313). University of Illinois Press.
  The lectures cite a 2023 Routledge reprint, in *Imperialism*, pp. 171–219. Lecture 4's citation is missing the title. The PDF link points to a University of Athens staff page; post a library copy on Canvas instead. The original appeared from University of Illinois Press, a nice local connection.

## Session 5 · Structure, signposting and scholarly communities

The syllabus says only "sociology-of-knowledge reading to be selected". These are suggestions:

- **Required, suggested (signposting).** Hyland, K. (2005). *Metadiscourse: Exploring interaction in writing*. Continuum. One chapter on the interpersonal model of metadiscourse.
  This is the direct source for Task 5's "framing and metadiscourse".
- **Required, suggested (sociology of knowledge).** Berger, P. L., & Luckmann, T. (1966). *The social construction of reality: A treatise in the sociology of knowledge*. Doubleday. Introduction only.
- **Optional, suggested.** Swales, J. M. (1990). *Genre analysis: English in academic and research settings*. Cambridge University Press. Discourse communities chapter.

## Session 6 · Reflexivity, positionality and AI in scholarship

The syllabus says "reflection reading to be selected". These are suggestions:

- **Required, suggested.** Haraway, D. (1988). Situated knowledges: The science question in feminism and the privilege of partial perspective. *Feminist Studies, 14*(3), 575–599. https://doi.org/10.2307/3178066
- **Required, suggested.** Bender, E. M., Gebru, T., McMillan-Major, A., & Shmitchell, S. (2021). On the dangers of stochastic parrots: Can language models be too big? In *Proceedings of the 2021 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency* (pp. 610–623). https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922
  This pairs positionality with the AI-use statement in Task 6.
- **Optional, suggested.** Harding, S. (1992). Rethinking standpoint epistemology: What is "strong objectivity"? *The Centennial Review, 36*(3), 437–470.

## Session 7 · The future of scholarship and final review workshop

The syllabus says "selected case on automation". The workshop should stay light:

- **Required, suggested.** Messeri, L., & Crockett, M. J. (2024). Artificial intelligence and illusions of understanding in scientific research. *Nature, 627*, 49–58. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0
- **Optional, suggested.** Bush, V. (1945). As we may think. *The Atlantic Monthly, 176*(1), 101–108.
  This is a historical counterpoint for "The Automatic Academy?".
