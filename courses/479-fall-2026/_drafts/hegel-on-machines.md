## Framing question

Hegel never saw a thinking machine and would have denied one was possible. Yet
he wrote about machines more often, and far more ambivalently, than his
reputation suggests. This session asks what happens to his account of spirit
when the instrument stops being a plough and starts being a model.

## 1. The tool outlasts the deed

**Sources:** *Jena Realphilosophie* (1803–06); *Science of Logic*, "Teleology"

Hegel's first move is to make the *tool*, not the labour, the philosophically
interesting thing. The tool is the rational middle term (*Mitte*) between the
worker and the object. Labour passes away and the product is consumed, but the
tool persists — and accumulates rationality as it does.

In the Logic he puts it memorably: the plough is more honourable than the
enjoyments it procures, because those enjoyments are forgotten while the
instrument endures.

**Why it matters here:** this is the seed of externalized memory. What a
civilisation knows is deposited in its instruments, not in its satisfactions.

## 2. Cunning that rebounds

**Source:** *Jena Realphilosophie II* (1805–06)

Much darker. The human uses *List* — cunning — against nature, interposing a
machine so that nature wears itself out doing the work. But Hegel says this
deception takes its revenge: the more thoroughly nature is subjugated, the
lower the human sinks. Labour becomes progressively "dead" labour, and as its
value falls its quantity rises.

**Note for students:** this is roughly forty years before Marx's "Fragment on
Machines," and Marx had read it.

## 3. The human steps aside

**Source:** *Philosophy of Right* §198 (see also §§196–197; §243 on the rabble)

The division of labour makes production progressively more abstract and more
mechanical, until at last the human being can step aside and let a machine take
their place.

Hegel presents this fairly coolly, as a moment in the system of needs. But §243,
on the poverty produced by industrial wealth, shows he knew the bill came due.

## 4. Mechanism cannot explain spirit

**Source:** *Science of Logic*, "Objectivity" — Mechanism

Hegel's official position. Mechanism is the *lowest* category of objectivity:
external relation, parts indifferent to one another. Life cannot be mechanism;
spirit certainly cannot. To apply mechanical categories to mind is a category
error.

Hold this in mind — the next section contradicts it.

---

## 5. The strange exception: mechanical memory

**Source:** *Encyclopaedia*, Philosophy of Mind §§461–464 (esp. §463)

This is the passage to build the seminar around.

When Hegel reaches *Gedächtnis* — rote memory, learning sequences of names by
heart, holding meaningless strings in order — he says, startlingly, that here
spirit exists *as* a mechanism. Not resembles. Is. And that this is a
**necessary** moment: the very hinge on which memory passes over into Thought.

So the one place Hegel concedes that spirit is machinery is precisely where a
system holds vast sequences of tokens without meaning — and his claim is that
thought emerges *through* that, not despite it.

Derrida works this passage hard in "The Pit and the Pyramid."

**Coda:** §410 — habit as the "mechanism of self-feeling," where the body
becomes a machine-like instrument the soul no longer attends to.

> memorisation is not the opposite of understanding but its precondition.

## Discussion questions

1. If the tool is where rationality is deposited and preserved, what is
   deposited in a trained model — and who can retrieve it?
2. Hegel's cunning rebounds on the one who exercises it. Who is being cunning
   in the case of a language model, and on whom does it rebound?
3. §463 says spirit *is* mechanism at the moment of rote memory. Does that make
   a language model a moment of spirit, or does it only tell us something about
   human memory?
4. Mechanical memory passes over into thought. What would the equivalent
   passage look like for a machine — and would we recognise it?

## Caveats to state in class

- **Section numbers drift between editions.** Verify against the edition
  students are using before citing.
- **The *Jena* material is unpublished lecture drafts**, not work Hegel released.
  Cite it as such.
- **The machine-as-cunning passages predate the *Phenomenology*** — this is
  early Hegel, not the mature system. The *Encyclopaedia* material is late.
