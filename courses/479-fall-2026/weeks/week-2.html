Overview

This week asks what it means to learn through experience. For Hegel, experience is not just a stream of sensations. Consciousness acts with some understanding of the world, discovers that the world does not fit that understanding, and is compelled to revise its own terms.

Machine learning systems also change in response to data and feedback, but it is an open question whether this amounts to experience. A model may update parameters or carry conversational state without having a first-person point of view. The distinction between functional change and lived experience will guide the week.

By the end of the week, you should be able to describe experience as a structure of expectation, encounter, contradiction, and revision, and explain why this definition complicates simple claims that machines either obviously do or obviously do not experience.

Questions for discussion

  1. Is experience something that happens to a learner, something a learner does, or both?
  2. Why does contradiction matter to experience?
  3. Is memory necessary for experience? Is a first-person point of view necessary?
  4. What evidence could distinguish an AI system that changes state from one that has experience?

How does experience relate to machine learning?

A machine-learning system encounters training examples, errors, rewards, prompts, and environmental feedback. These can alter parameters, retrieved context, or subsequent action. This resembles the functional structure of learning from experience, but resemblance does not settle whether the machine has a felt or self-interpreting experience. We must state which meaning of “experience” we are using.

How does experience relate to human learning?

Human learners bring expectations, histories, bodily capacities, emotions, and social positions to an encounter. Experience becomes educational when a mismatch is noticed and worked through rather than ignored. Reflection converts an event into a reason to revise a belief, habit, or practice.

Self-assessed weekly activity: Experience audit

1. Begin with a machine-human chat

Start a new chat with this prompt:

Help me investigate the concept of experience. Ask me to describe one recent learning experience as a sequence of expectation, encounter, contradiction, and revision. Then describe, in strictly functional terms, what changes within this chat as you respond. Clearly separate claims about observable operation from claims you cannot establish about subjective experience.

Continue until you have a human case and a machine case expressed in the same four-part sequence.

2. Produce an artifact

Create a two-column experience audit comparing the human and machine cases. For each, identify:

  • what existed before the encounter;
  • what new input or resistance appeared;
  • what changed afterward;
  • whether the change persisted; and
  • what cannot be inferred from the available evidence.

Add a 200-word conclusion answering: Does functional revision count as experience?

3. Reflect

  • Did the machine make any unsupported claims about itself?
  • Which features of your own experience were visible in behavior, and which were only reportable from the first-person perspective?
  • What observation would make you revise your conclusion?
